### Went to a Korean fried chicken place and found it full of Chinese people

People's awkwardness often doesn't come from others' coldness, but from their expectations of how others will respond not being met.

### Simple and complex

I'm more and more drawn to simplicity — it can be pure simplicity, or an abstract kind of simple concision.

But I like spending time with complex people, not because simple is bad, but because complex people carry more real traces of life — struggle, contradiction, uncertainty, anxiety over gain and loss, insecurity. Still the same definition: simple -> complex -> simple. But the closer you get to the truth, the harder it is to say it clearly in simple words. That's where the world's complexity sits. So at this point we hit a contradiction: do we still need to pursue simplicity? Simplicity is a philosophy of life — the great way is simple. Laozi used the Tao Te Ching, expressing extremely deep philosophy in extremely spare language. Steve Jobs presented complex technology through the most concise user interface. Simple doesn't mean easy.

Where is that sense of boundary? It comes back to understanding and applying balance. A few examples I can recall:

Not packaging myself. I know perfection itself is a kind of falseness. Exposing inner contradictions — real honesty is far more precious than surface clarity. A sincere simplicity.

Admitting ignorance. The more you understand yourself and this world, the more you find that the closest thing to a real answer is often: I still don't understand.

This is still a philosophy of life, a concise aesthetics of depth.

### Information = a trigger for structural perturbation

> 2025-06-07 12:22:05 · `#人生/成长`

Information != capability; information = a trigger for structural perturbation

It suddenly occurred to me that there was a question before that was cut off short and never got an answer. This sentence suddenly made me think back to that question.

If experience represents growth, then meaningful experience can bring a better life and better growth. A friend said before that even some bad experiences, being deceived for instance, are meaningful to him — at least he learned not to casually trust anyone again. But for me, I feel that if I were to go through that kind of experience again and choose it again, it would still be meaningless. These things can all be answered in information or in other people's growth experiences; essentially they're all a process of understanding human nature, and these are unchanging and learnable without having to experience them yourself. So I wonder whether some bad experiences also make a person worse, and whether such experiences are truly meaningless.

The key here is the irreplaceability of experience.

Since other people's experiences can serve as information input, why do we still need to go through that pain ourselves? Why can't we learn the complexity of human nature directly from others' lessons?

Where exactly is the difference between emotional cognition and rational cognition?

When you learn the information "people can deceive" from someone else's story, what you gain is knowledge at the rational level. But when you are deceived yourself, what you gain is a bodily, emotional understanding — the shock of trust shattering, the process of re-evaluating the world, the experience of rebuilding your standards of judgment amid confusion.

These two kinds of cognition play completely different roles in your "structural perturbation." The former may just add one item to your knowledge base; the latter may reshape your basic perceptual framework for the world.

But the same experience is a double-edged sword. Of those who were deceived, some become wiser, some become paranoid; of those who lost in love, some learn to cherish, some close their hearts; of those whose startup failed, some start again, some never dare take a risk again...

But it seems I asked the wrong question. The question isn't "should I actively seek experience," but "how do I stay aware within experience."

Those whom experience "makes worse" usually passively absorb the impact, and then let emotion and fear dominate the subsequent cognitive reconstruction. Those whom it "makes better" more often keep some kind of observer's perspective within the experience, observing their own reactions and thinking about the reasons behind them.

The point may not be to avoid every experience that might make you "worse," but to cultivate the ability to stay clear-headed and reflective in any experience.

Then let's look at the topic of information again — information is like this too. Information is a trigger for structure and action; some information may threaten you, some may be holding you back. Information still belongs to the old framework, the process of simple -> complex -> simple.

Modern people are like someone standing under an information waterfall, desperately opening their mouth to drink the "useful" droplets, and ending up half-choked.

What is the essence of information? To understand it through LLMs: the brain's input — is a model's power only about how much content is fed in? No. The brain itself is a filter — the key isn't how much information you collect, but how good your filter is.

Information must ultimately become knowledge, thinking, insight, even translate into action. We're not after "knowing more," but after "thinking deeper."

What's truly valuable isn't the massive volume of information input from outside, but the information that can react chemically with your inner cognitive structure.

Information ≠ knowledge ≠ wisdom ≠ action

### Two apps, two essences: guides vs. journals

> 2025-06-09 22:39:41

Setting aside magical community apps like Xiaohongshu, which can do both guides and journals (essentially, the value comes from sharing)

For the other vertical apps, the difference between the guide type and the journal type

The most essential thing is that one is a subtraction product and the other is an addition product.

Travel/living guide app (subtraction product):

It solves the anxiety of "not knowing what to do"

It helps users reduce uncertainty, reduce trial-and-error cost, reduce the burden of planning

User's goal: don't make mistakes

The value peak is before the trip; use and discard

Travel journal app (addition product):

It solves the fear of "not wanting to be forgotten"

It helps users add meaning, add memories, add self-knowledge

User's goal: it has to be meaningful

Value increases with time; the more you use it, the more precious it becomes

Design philosophy:

Subtraction type: completing the goal as the means, arranging the upcoming itinerary; it satisfies safety needs and is driven by anxiety.

Addition type: user experience, deep feeling, a sense of meaning. Driven by self-actualization and meaning; emotional barriers and a data moat.

Guide app:

Easily disrupted by AI (ChatGPT can already make guides)

Low user loyalty, low switching cost

Monetization relies on ads and commissions

Fierce competition, a red ocean market

Journal app:

Hard to replace; the data is the moat

High user loyalty, high switching cost

Can monetize through subscriptions

Mild competition, a blue ocean market

### Wanting is essentially dopamine-driven

> 2025-06-10 10:06:36 · `#情绪变化`

Liking a product and paying for it — but the subconscious act of paying: saying "like" for it seems too big a word. The more accurate word to describe it is "want." I crave getting this product, this object.

Setting aside biological instinctive craving, when dopamine levels rise we crave a particular thing more.

The absolute level of dopamine is only one side of it; what matters more is the "relative change." Short videos, for example: sudden happiness, but it's hard to get happier. A fairly high dopamine level makes it hard to produce a larger change.

So when you keep doing something you like, the threshold at which you feel happiness keeps rising.

The total "reserve" of dopamine is limited! However the happiness is obtained — through work, exercise or other means — the happiness currency being spent is the same one: dopamine.

Here we can understand a more subconscious function of anti-addiction mechanisms: if we always keep the same happiness response to the same thing, we might stop exploring as individuals. Proper restraint and limits can make happiness more precious.

Dopamine can have a stacking effect, driving the peak higher. The higher the dopamine peak, the longer the sustained trough that follows, and over time this may reduce your ability to get happiness from exercise alone.

The dopamine reserve is also limited, which means we need to budget our pursuit of pleasure wisely.

Sustained high stimulation causes dopamine receptors to be "downregulated"

### The levels of rebuttal and sincere tolerance

> 2025-06-19 16:12:56

Why do some people seem to hate being contradicted

Rebuttal isn't a derogatory word; it's a neutral word, involving reason, sensibility and intuition, and expression.

It's driven by human instinct. My dad also dislikes me contradicting him. Essentially it's a failure to understand each other in communication, a combination of human instinct's self-protection mechanism and cognitive bias.

Paul Graham, author of Hackers & Painters and YC founder: there are seven levels of rebuttal

(Level one) Name-calling: the lowest, pure emotional attack (like "you're an idiot").

(Level two) Ad hominem: not discussing the content, attacking the other's character or right to speak (like "you have no right to speak").

(Level three) Criticizing tone: not addressing the content of the statement, only criticizing the manner of expression (like "your attitude is terrible").

(Level four) Contrarianism: offering a counter-argument with almost no support (like "I don't believe it, you're making it up").

(Level five) Offering a counter-argument: rebutting with logic or evidence (like "the data shows the opposite").

(Level six) Attempting falsification: pointing out specific fallacies and rebutting with citations.

(Level seven) Falsifying the main thesis: the highest level, directly dismantling the other's core argument.

---

Understanding and tolerance are completely different — two separate things.

I once ran into a situation where a person, toward gay people, in order to keep their own poise and magnanimity, said: I don't understand it, I don't agree with it, but I respect it.

I've said before: tolerance without understanding isn't real tolerance, and easily becomes a formality or collapses in practice. Understanding doesn't equal agreement, but it's the bridge to sincere respect, and sincerity and respect are especially important in any relationship.

If you choose silence in the face of clearly wrong information, harmful views or unfair situations, that isn't tolerance but indifference or evasion. So tolerance not based on understanding is problematic. I understand what they're performing — meeting society's expectation of tolerance, or treating tolerance as capital, as a culture to show off, as a display tool. ### When "understanding" fails, switch on "trust" mode

> 2025-06-21 00:03:29 · `#人生`

Understanding "understanding" and "tolerance" deeply once more (with some playful AI polish added after the chat)

At first we were just talking about the two words "tolerance" and "indulgence." One character apart — how big a deal could it be? But as we talked, it turned out this is the most fundamental question of connection between people.

"Tolerance" sounds beautiful, but its root has to be "understanding." Tolerance without understanding is like a house with no foundation — either a cold "I can't be bothered with you," or a principle-less "do whatever you want." That isn't tolerance; that's letting things go, even indulgence.

If you want to understand, you need "empathy." But what is "empathy"? Empathy isn't sympathy. Sympathy feels a bit like standing on the shore watching someone struggle in the water and throwing them a lifebuoy, saying "Hey buddy, that's rough, hang in there!" That's a distant kind of care, even a slightly condescending one.

But "empathy" is different. It's jumping into the water yourself, staying by their side, feeling the same cold water temperature, and saying: "Damn, this water is f***ing cold. I'm here with you — let's figure out how to get to shore." It's "I understand your cold," not "I know you're cold."

It's simply impossible for people to understand each other 100%. Because our experiences are unique.

So this rises to a philosophical level: each of us is, essentially, lonely. We all live in a world built from our own unique senses and memories. We can never truly "become" the other person and feel everything they feel. This is our growth, and our task.

Growth note one: give up the fixation on "perfect understanding." Expecting to find someone who understands me at every "point" is an unrealistic fantasy. Treat that fantasy as the cornerstone of an intimate relationship and the house will collapse sooner or later — understanding alone just isn't enough.

Growth note two: when "understanding" fails, switch on "trust" mode. This is one of people's own precious qualities — being willing to trust someone. A sentence ten thousand times more important than "I understand you" is: "I don't understand, but I believe you."

Everything happening in your world — even if I can't see it with my own eyes, I believe it exists. I won't deny your reality because of my own ignorance. I'm by your side; I support you.

Growth note three: a relationship's strength isn't about overlap but about the ability to "build bridges." Whether a relationship is solid depends not on how much two people's experiences overlap, but on whether, in those areas that can't overlap and are full of distance, both sides are willing and able to build a bridge together.

In the end, it seems what we're looking for may not be a "soulmate" but a "soul ally." A comrade willing, in the moments when I can't be understood, to still stand by my side and say "I don't understand, but I'm here."

That may be far more realistic, and far more precious, than finding someone who "understands everything."

### The time-zone problem in engineering

> 2025-06-06 12:05:10 · `#领域`

The backend only ever recognizes UTC

The most core, most universal, least error-prone design philosophy is this: at the level of data storage (the database) and business logic (the backend), always use UTC (Coordinated Universal Time) only. The work of converting and displaying time zones is handed entirely to the front end, which sits closest to the user.

The database part: the database is time's final storage place. Its core duty is to record a point in time accurately and without error, and it doesn't care what that point looks like in any particular time zone.

Use a time-zone-aware data type: preferably TIMESTAMP WITH TIME ZONE (in PostgreSQL abbreviated as TIMESTAMPTZ). This takes the time zone information you provide when inserting data and converts it into a standard UTC timestamp for storage. When you query it, it converts and displays according to the current time zone setting of your database session, but what it stores underneath is always a unique, unambiguous UTC time. This eliminates ambiguity at the root.

The backend stores only the user's time zone preference. In the user profile, add a field to store the user's time zone identifier, for example America/Los_Angeles or Asia/Shanghai.

The front end's job is mainly to turn the cold UTC time into local time that the user is familiar with and can easily understand.

### Changes in aesthetic threshold and perceptual granularity

### Two methods - the context promotion strategy

> 2025-06-13 17:20:56 · `#格物/feed` `#格物/AI`

Promote the dynamic data of a one-off task (an article) into a static background for the duration of the session (part of the system instructions) when entering a multi-turn conversation, so that the expensive computation cost for the whole document is paid only once, and all subsequent follow-up questions can get ultra-fast, low-cost responses through prefix caching.

Stage one: initial processing (one-off task)

Goal: do a first pass of processing on any article (like generating a summary).

Configuration:

System Prompt: contains general, unchanging instructions ("You are a summarization assistant...").

User Prompt: contains the full text of the article, which changes dynamically.

Caching logic: what's cached at this stage is the general "instructions," suitable for processing many different articles.

Stage two: follow-up session (stateful conversation)

Goal: do deep, multi-turn Q&A about the same article.

Configuration (at session start):

System Prompt: dynamically constructed, with the content "general instructions + full article text." This enormous prompt stays unchanged for the current session.

User Prompt: contains only the user's first follow-up question.

Caching logic:

First follow-up: the API processes this enormous System Prompt and writes its complete computation result (KV Cache) into the cache. This call has higher latency and higher cost.

Subsequent follow-ups: the System Prompt stays unchanged, the API detects a perfect prefix match, and loads its full understanding of the article directly from cache. The model only needs to process the new, short follow-up question. Latency is extremely low and cost drops dramatically.
