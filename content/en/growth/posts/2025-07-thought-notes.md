What did it change, or what did it reveal that was always there

The Tale of Genji shaped the tonal foundation of Japanese aesthetics, but rather than saying these concepts were created by Genji, it's more that it expressed what Japan felt deep down about impermanence, incompleteness, the beauty of things that are gone in a flash.

Just as Murasaki Shikibu wrote over a thousand years ago, life is like a dream; the times change, but humanity's confusion about existence itself never changes.

The reason The Tale of Genji could influence Japanese culture for a thousand years is precisely that it touched some shared human experience that transcends the era.

A great work is both the deepest expression of its age and the bravest breakthrough past the age's limits. It's not either/or, it's two sides of the same coin.

The question really worth thinking about might be: in our age, what kind of creation can both respond deeply to the present and touch the eternal in human nature?

### Martin Buber talked about "I and Thou" versus "I and It"

> 2025-07-21 14:33:33

I actually never understood it — it's just that the object you face is "you," not "it."

Actually it seems their most important relationship is how we ultimately meet this world.

The "I-It" relationship is the mode we're most familiar with day to day; in this relationship most "Its" are objects to be observed, analyzed and used — clients, users, influencers, investors.

The "I-Thou" relationship is completely different. It's an encounter of the whole person; in this relationship there's no intermediary and no purpose, you don't need to observe the other, you co-create a special existence.

One is getting information, one is getting experience.

Making products is like this too: do you treat the user as a market, as a "Thou," as a friend? If you only rationally analyze data and analyze metrics, you really will lose a lot of what users truly care about.

Are we building a relationship with the world, or conquering the world? Are we creating connection, or manufacturing separation?

Causal analysis isn't rational analysis; it's a comforting story the human brain weaves to cope with chaos.

Logical analysis and causal analysis may be two different things. Logical analysis cares about consistency between concepts, while causal analysis cares about connectivity between events. But connectivity itself may be a cognitive illusion of humans.

We never truly "see" causality, we only see events following one after another, and then habitually think the former caused the latter. Causality may be nothing more than a way our brain interprets chronological order.

Causal analysis isn't describing reality, it's creating a useful model of reality. It isn't discovering truth, it's building tools. When we say "A causes B," what we're really saying is "if you want B, then doing A may be effective."

### Discovered, Not Designed

> 2025-07-07 17:44:49

"Discovered, Not Designed" — design is dead, discovery lives forever

Traditional design methods rely on reductionism, that is, understanding the whole by understanding the parts of a system. However, in complex systems the whole is greater than the sum of the parts. The macro behavior of a system (like the flight patterns of a flock of birds, the nest-building behavior of an ant colony) isn't decided by any single individual. It sounds absurd, but it's actually the nonlinear interaction between a large number of individuals based on simple rules.

Embrace discovery; McClure advocates a "Meta-Design" or "discovery-driven" approach. Under this paradigm, we no longer directly design the final behavior of the system, we design an "environment" or "rule set" that promotes the emergence of the desired behavior. Then, through observation, experimentation and iteration, we "discover" and select the systems that show the properties we want. This is more like a gardener carefully tending the soil and environment so plants grow naturally, rather than mechanically assembling a car.

Actually designing AI systems seems like this too: don't focus excessively on the AI system learning specific skills, but learn to let AI emerge, define rules, and embrace failure and surprise.

### The essence of memory: between reconstruction and replay

> 2025-07-18 14:04:10

What is the essence of memory in the human brain?

Does the essence of memory in the human brain lie in a dynamic combination between reconstruction and replay?

Memory is stored in a distributed way in the connection patterns between neurons (synaptic strength); for example a childhood memory may be distributed across different brain regions for vision, hearing and emotion (happiness).

For events that just happened, people may also miss details, like where objects in a room were placed, so memory can't extract the original information as it was; it's more like piecing things together from fragments.

Reconstruction is memory's most central property; every recall counts as re-creation.

Memory retrieval is essentially a reconstruction process based on existing information and experience: through some elements, activating a judgment about the original memory, then automatically filling in details with present-day knowledge, emotions or suggestion, or even adding extra details.

So a person in an angry state, recalling a conflict, will emphasize the other person's "aggressiveness" more; this is emotion distorting memory reconstruction.

Memory reconstruction isn't completely detached from reality; it uses the original experience as an "anchor," and only processes the details.

Especially details that aren't solidly fixed, like background music, what people were wearing; these are more easily reconstructed.

The purpose of reconstruction is to save cognitive resources, adapt to new environments, and reduce storage burden.

So the essence of memory is a dynamic process; every recall is a mixed product of original fragments, the current environment and personal experience, like assembling a puzzle from fragments — each time the picture that comes out is similar but not identical.

### The core definition of a dual economic structure

> 2025-07-31 11:42:43

A dual economic structure usually exists in developing countries, or cities in the middle of modernization: two utterly different economic sectors, differing greatly in resource allocation, production efficiency and income levels.

Three stages:

Unlimited labour supply,

the modern sector uses its profits for reinvestment and expands production scale, absorbing further labour, forming a "profit → investment → employment → more profit" cycle.

Change in the traditional sector: the outflow of labour doesn't affect agricultural output (because the marginal productivity is zero), and rural income stays at subsistence level.

Labour shortage appears (the "Lewis turning point").

The marginal productivity of the traditional sector starts to rise: as labour decreases, agricultural production has to improve efficiency (adopting new technology, for instance), rural income rises accordingly, and the urban-rural gap narrows.

The two sectors merge (the "commercialization stage").

Wage levels in the traditional and modern sectors converge, labour movement between the two sectors is decided by market mechanisms (marginal productivity), the dual structure disappears, and the economy enters a "unified" stage of development.

### Latecomer advantage and latecomer disadvantage

> 2025-07-31 11:53:26

It refers to countries or companies that started relatively late being able, in the course of development, to use the technology, experience, management models and even institutions that the forerunners already explored, thereby avoiding detours and achieving development, even catching up, at lower cost and faster speed.

For example: the rise of Japan and South Korea.

Latecomer disadvantage is one of the important reasons many countries fall into the middle-income trap.

Many developing countries, after reaching middle-income level, see economic growth slow or even stall, and fail to successfully enter the ranks of high-income countries.

The main cause is excessive reliance on imitation and a lack of independent innovation.

### High-altitude apparent temperature is usually very low, because it's far from the surface layer

> 2025-07-17 11:20:12

The atmosphere's main direct heat source is radiation from the ground, not direct radiation from the sun.

So although high altitude still gets direct sunlight, the temperature isn't very high, and a big part of the reason is that the atmosphere's insulation effect is weakened.

But the burning feeling on your skin is very obvious: the air at high altitude is thin, the atmosphere's weakening effect on solar radiation is small, so the direct solar radiation reaching the surface, especially ultraviolet radiation, is very strong.

This also seems to explain why there's such a thing as two different heavens on the same day: after the sun sets the temperature drops abruptly, because the air lacks effective heat storage.
