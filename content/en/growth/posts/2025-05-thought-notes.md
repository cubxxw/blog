### Some new thoughts on flow

> 2025-05-03 19:07:50 · `#人生`

"Anxiety is thinking without restraint. Flow is restraint without thinking." — James Clear, habits researcher

I've discussed a finding before: I feel I reach a flow state more easily when I'm slightly tipsy, or when I'm sleepy. This phenomenon baffles me.

Humans perform at their best precisely when they enter a thought-free, self-forgetting state. Japanese has a beautiful adjective for this state: mushin (no-mind).

No-mind means no distracting thoughts in consciousness, no resentment or anger, no worry or fear, to the point of no self.

Only with the mind free of distractions can you escape the limits of the self and create all the variety in this world. I don't want to force this belief on you, I only hope you're willing to try it and experience it, and grasp it through your own experience.

It may be the same as that teacher earlier who forgot his lesson plan between classes — the same way, he reached a flow state more easily.

So the essence of anxiety is thinking too much, and the essence of flow is having no distractions.

So back to an earlier question: why do I more easily reach a flow state or a focused state when tipsy, or in a tired state?

When tipsy, the prefrontal cortex (PFC) — the region in charge of planning, criticism and self-monitoring — has its activity mildly suppressed by alcohol, which is a bit like the "ego dissolution" effect after meditation.

Tiredness may be a bit different: executive function declines -> less criticism/distraction, similar to the flow effect, but actually it's because resources are insufficient and the brain automatically processes in "energy-saving" mode, only focusing on the current task and no longer handling multi-tasking interference.

It may "look" easier to enter flow, but in substance it's entering a "narrowed attention" state, not genuinely efficient flow.

Usually early morning is the golden time when it's easier to enter genuinely "high-quality flow": the brain has just restored its resources, and executive function is at its most complete (including working memory, suppressing distraction, emotion regulation).

This may be why artists all need an environment, music and a little wine to activate their artistic cells.

### The mechanism of emotional transference

> 2025-05-17 17:56:50 · `#人生`

I said before that China's development needs to be viewed through a generational lens, because the gaps between each generation's upbringing are enormous.

There's a type of person whose childhood environment brought distrust and insecurity, giving rise to a strong desire for control, transferring inner unease onto the people around him. The core of this is really the nature of emotional transference.

The security he wants is something he tries to grip tightly by controlling other people, but that security is fake, because it's built on other people's lack of freedom.

What is the nature of emotional transference? Put plainly, it's projecting the unease and fear inside onto the outside, trying to make yourself feel better by changing other people.

This pattern is far too common in human nature; all of us have done something like it more or less — losing your temper at family when you're anxious, or gripping tighter when you fear losing something. (It just depends on whether we become aware of it; this is a bit like metacognition, the eye of God.)

Human nature is essentially chasing three things: security, belonging, self-actualization.

But ironically, security often becomes the limit; this is precisely what blocks his path to self-actualization. Real growth needs freedom and trust, but he puts shackles on himself and on other people alike.

Human nature is this complex and this fragile. We're all afraid of getting hurt, but the more afraid we are the tighter we grip, and the tighter we grip the more we hurt, until finally we push ourselves and everyone else into a corner. The real balance is learning to accept your own imperfections, and daring to believe the world isn't that dangerous, even if you trip now and then.

The basic model service generates the text summary, summarizing according to the text's type and length.

Summaries generally come in two kinds: abstractive summarization and extractive summarization.

It allows the model, when generating each summary word, to attend to the relevant part of the original text.
